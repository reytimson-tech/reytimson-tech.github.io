Anatomy of a strong prompt

Most disappointing AI output can be traced to a missing ingredient. This module gives you a simple recipe for strong prompts.

Learning objectives

  • Name the six ingredients of a strong prompt
  • Rewrite weak prompts into strong ones
  • Use positive instructions and explain why
  • Use roles appropriately
  • Apply a reusable prompt template

2.1 Six ingredients

Most strong prompts combine some or all of these ingredients. You won't need every one every time — but when output disappoints, the missing ingredient is usually the cause.

IngredientAsk yourselfExample
RoleWhat expertise or perspective should it bring?"You are an experienced UK employment lawyer…"
TaskWhat exactly should it do? Use a clear verb."Review this contract clause and list risks for the employer."
ContextWho's it for? Why? What background matters?"The reader is a small business owner with no legal training."
FormatWhat should the output look like?"A table with columns: risk, why it matters, suggested wording."
ConstraintsLength, tone, language, what to avoid"Under 300 words. UK English. No legal jargon."
ExamplesCan I show what good looks like?"Here's a summary we liked last month: …"

2.2 Before and after

Weak

"Write a LinkedIn post about our new product."

Strong

"You're a B2B marketer. Write a LinkedIn post announcing Bamboo Prep, our new range of bamboo chopping boards. Audience: independent kitchenware retailers in the UK. Key points: FSC-certified bamboo, knife-friendly, wholesale discount for first orders before 30 November. Tone: warm and practical, not salesy. 120–150 words, UK English, max two emojis, end with a question to encourage comments."

2.3 Be explicit about what you want

  • Say what to do, not only what to avoid. "Write in short, plain sentences" works better than "Don't be verbose".
  • Explain why. "Keep it under 100 words because it will be read on a phone" helps the model make sensible trade-offs.
  • Specify the audience and purpose. A summary for a board differs from one for new starters.
  • Ask for the level of ambition you want. If you want a thorough, creative or opinionated result, say so.

2.4 Using roles well

Giving the model a role — in the system prompt or at the start — focuses its tone, vocabulary and priorities. It's most useful when you want a particular perspective: "Review this as a sceptical CFO would." A role is not a substitute for context, though. "You are a marketing expert" adds less than three sentences about your actual customers.

2.5 A reusable template

ROLE: You are a [role] helping [who].

TASK: [Clear verb + what to produce].

CONTEXT:
- Audience: [who will read/use it]
- Purpose: [what it's for / decision it supports]
- Background: [anything the model needs to know]

FORMAT: [structure, headings, table, bullet points, length]

CONSTRAINTS: [tone, UK English, things to avoid, must-include points]

If anything important is unclear, ask me up to 3 questions before you start.

The last line is a powerful habit: it lets the model surface gaps in your brief before it guesses.

Try it: upgrade a prompt

Take this weak prompt — "Help me prepare for my appraisal" — and rewrite it using the template. Run both in an approved AI tool and compare the results.

Key takeaways

  • Role, task, context, format, constraints and examples.
  • Say what to do, explain why, and name the audience.
  • Roles focus perspective but don't replace context.
  • Invite clarifying questions before the model starts.
