What AI governance is

Every organisation using AI needs to know what AI it has, who's responsible, and how risks are managed. That's AI governance.

Learning objectives

  • Define AI governance and the questions it answers
  • Explain why AI governance is urgent now
  • Compare centralised, federated and decentralised operating models
  • Describe key governance roles
  • Apply the three lines model to AI

1.1 What AI governance is

AI governance is the system of structures, policies, processes and controls an organisation uses to make sure its AI is developed, procured and used in line with its values, its risk appetite and the law. IBM puts it simply: the processes, standards and guardrails that help ensure AI systems are safe and ethical.

Governance answers practical questions:

  • What AI are we using, and where? (inventory)
  • Which uses are acceptable, and which are not? (policy and risk appetite)
  • Who decides, and who is accountable? (roles and decision rights)
  • How do we assess and manage risks before and after launch? (risk management)
  • How do we show we're compliant? (documentation, assurance and audit)

1.2 Why now

  • AI is everywhere: it arrives through new features in existing software, staff using generative AI tools, and vendors — not just in-house data science projects.
  • Regulation is arriving: the EU AI Act's obligations are phasing in through 2028; regulators worldwide are applying existing laws to AI.
  • Customers and partners ask: procurement questionnaires increasingly ask about AI governance, and certifications such as ISO/IEC 42001 are becoming a differentiator.
  • Risk is real: data leakage, discrimination, inaccurate outputs, IP infringement, security vulnerabilities and reputational damage.

1.3 Governance enables, not just restricts

Good governance makes it easier to use AI: clear rules mean teams know what's allowed, low-risk uses go through quickly, and leaders can approve ambitious projects with confidence. The aim is proportionate governance — light-touch for low-risk uses, rigorous for high-risk ones.

1.4 Operating models

ModelHow it worksSuits
CentralisedA central AI governance team reviews and approves all AI usesSmaller organisations, early stages, highly regulated sectors
Federated (hub and spoke)A central hub sets standards and handles high-risk cases; business units have trained "spokes" who handle routine reviewsLarger, diverse organisations — the most common mature model
DecentralisedEach business unit governs its own AI against shared principlesRare; risks inconsistency

1.5 Key roles

RoleResponsibilities
Board / executive sponsorSets AI strategy and risk appetite; receives reporting; ultimately accountable
AI governance committee or councilCross-functional body (legal, risk, privacy, security, technology, business, HR) that sets policy and reviews high-risk cases
Head of AI governance / responsible AI leadRuns the programme day to day
AI system ownerAccountable for a specific system throughout its life
Builders and buyersFollow lifecycle requirements; document and test
Internal auditIndependent assurance that governance works

1.6 The three lines model

Many organisations map AI governance onto the Institute of Internal Auditors' three lines model:

  1. First line: teams that build, buy and use AI own and manage its risks.
  2. Second line: risk, compliance, privacy and security functions set frameworks, advise and challenge.
  3. Third line: internal audit provides independent assurance to the board.

Key takeaways

  • Governance covers inventory, policy, roles, risk management and assurance.
  • AI arrives through vendors and features, not just in-house projects.
  • Good governance is proportionate — and speeds up safe adoption.
  • Federated hub-and-spoke models are common in larger organisations.
  • First line owns risk; second line advises and challenges; third line assures.
