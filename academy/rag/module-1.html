Why LLMs need your data

LLMs are brilliant generalists that know nothing about your organisation. Retrieval-augmented generation fixes that by looking things up first.

1.1 What an LLM doesn't know

A large language model is trained on a huge snapshot of public text. Out of the box it knows nothing about:

  • Your private information — policies, contracts, product specs, tickets, wikis.
  • Anything after its training cut-off — new prices, regulations, decisions.
  • Exact details it saw rarely — and it may confidently invent them (hallucinate).

Ask a general assistant "What's our refund window for damaged items?" and the best it can do is guess what companies usually do.

1.2 Three ways to give a model your knowledge

ApproachHow it worksGood forLimits
Put it in the promptPaste the relevant document into the contextOne-off questions; small document setsDoesn't scale to thousands of documents; cost grows with every token
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)Search your documents for the most relevant passages and put only those into the promptLarge, changing knowledge bases; answers with citationsOnly as good as the retrieval step
Fine-tuningFurther train the model on your examplesStyle, format, specialised behaviourPoor at adding facts that change; expensive to update (see the Fine-tuning course)

For "answer questions from our documents", RAG is usually the right starting point: it's cheaper, easier to update (just change the documents) and lets you show sources.

1.3 The RAG pipeline

Every RAG system has two halves:

  1. Indexing (done ahead of time): load documents → split them into chunks → turn each chunk into an embedding → store chunks and embeddings in an index.
  2. Query time (on every question): embed the question → retrieve the most similar chunks → put them into a prompt with the question → the LLM writes an answer grounded in those chunks, ideally with citations.

1.4 Where RAG goes wrong

When a RAG system gives a bad answer, the cause is usually one of these — and each module of this course tackles one:

Try it: is RAG the right tool?

For each, choose prompt-stuffing, RAG or fine-tuning: (a) an HR assistant answering from 400 policy documents updated monthly; (b) summarising one 20-page contract; (c) making every output follow your house style for product descriptions.

Show answers

(a) RAG — large, changing knowledge base. (b) Put it in the prompt — one document fits easily. (c) Fine-tuning (or a strong prompt with examples) — it's about style, not facts.

Key takeaways

  • LLMs don't know your private, recent or rare information.
  • RAG retrieves relevant passages and puts only those into the prompt.
  • RAG suits large, changing knowledge; fine-tuning suits style and behaviour.
  • Indexing happens ahead of time; retrieval and generation happen per question.
  • Most RAG failures come from embeddings, chunking, retrieval, grounding or missing evaluation.
