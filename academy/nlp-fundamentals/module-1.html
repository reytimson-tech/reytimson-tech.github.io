1.1 What is natural language processing?

Natural language processing (NLP) is the set of techniques that let computers work with human language: classifying messages, finding information, measuring sentiment, searching by meaning, translating, summarising and generating text. Large language models are the newest and most powerful NLP tools — but classic techniques remain fast, cheap and surprisingly strong for many business tasks.

TaskBusiness example
ClassificationRoute each customer email to billing, delivery or product teams
Sentiment analysisTrack whether reviews are getting more negative
Information extractionPull order numbers, dates and amounts out of messages
Semantic searchFind help articles that answer a question, even with different wording
Topic discoveryFind out what thousands of survey comments are about
GenerationDraft replies or summaries (usually with an LLM)

1.2 The practice dataset

You'll use 400 synthetic customer messages, each labelled delivery, product or billing. About 8% of the labels are deliberately wrong — real labels are never perfect.

Python · practice data: synthetic customer messages (run this first)import random
import re
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

random.seed(7)
templates = {
    "delivery": ["My parcel arrived {d} days late and the box was crushed",
                 "Delivery took {d} days and the courier left it in the rain",
                 "Still waiting for my order after {d} days, no update",
                 "The tracking link never updated and delivery was {d} days late"],
    "product":  ["The {p} broke after a week",
                 "Love the {p}, great quality and well made",
                 "The {p} is much smaller than the photos suggest",
                 "Excellent {p}, works perfectly"],
    "billing":  ["I was charged twice for order {o}",
                 "Refund for order {o} still not received",
                 "The discount code did not apply at checkout",
                 "Invoice for order {o} shows the wrong amount"],
}
products = ["chopping board", "kettle", "desk lamp", "backpack", "water bottle"]
rows = []
for i in range(400):
    cat = random.choice(list(templates))
    msg = random.choice(templates[cat]).format(
        d=random.randint(2, 9), p=random.choice(products), o="GFY-%05d" % random.randint(10000, 99999))
    label = cat if random.random() > 0.08 else random.choice(list(templates))  # ~8% noisy labels
    rows.append({"text": msg, "category": label})
messages = pd.DataFrame(rows)
print(messages.sample(5, random_state=1))
print(messages["category"].value_counts())

1.3 From text to tokens

Computers can't do arithmetic on sentences, so the first step is to break text into tokens — usually words or pieces of words — and often to normalise them (lowercase, remove punctuation).

def simple_tokens(text):
    text = text.lower()
    text = re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9\s-]", " ", text)   # keep letters, digits, spaces, hyphens
    return text.split()

print(simple_tokens("Refund for order GFY-12345 still NOT received!"))

Choices like these matter. Removing punctuation helps matching but can destroy meaning ("not" vs "not!" is harmless; "can't" becoming "can t" is not). Lowercasing merges "Apple" the company with "apple" the fruit. There's no universally right answer — check what your task needs.

1.4 Bag of words

The simplest way to turn text into numbers is a bag of words: count how often each word appears, ignoring order. Each message becomes a row; each word in the vocabulary becomes a column.

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer

docs = ["parcel arrived late", "refund not received", "parcel late again"]
cv = CountVectorizer()
counts = cv.fit_transform(docs)
print(pd.DataFrame(counts.toarray(), columns=cv.get_feature_names_out()))

Bag of words loses word order — "not good, bad" and "not bad, good" look identical — but for many classification tasks it works remarkably well.

1.5 Stop words and n-grams

Stop words are very common words ("the", "and", "for") that often add little meaning. N-grams keep short sequences of words together, so "not received" or "charged twice" become features in their own right — partly recovering word order.

cv2 = CountVectorizer(stop_words="english", ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=3)
X_counts = cv2.fit_transform(messages["text"])
print(X_counts.shape)                                  # (messages, features)
top = np.asarray(X_counts.sum(axis=0)).ravel()
print(pd.Series(top, index=cv2.get_feature_names_out()).sort_values(ascending=False).head(10))

Be careful with stop-word lists: scikit-learn's English list includes "not" and "no", which flip meaning in sentiment tasks.

Key takeaways

  • NLP covers classification, extraction, search, topics and generation.
  • Tokenisation and normalisation choices change meaning — test them.
  • Bag of words counts words and ignores order.
  • N-grams recover some word order; stop-word lists can remove 'not'.
