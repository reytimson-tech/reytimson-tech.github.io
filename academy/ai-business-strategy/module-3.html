Business cases and measuring value

AI investments compete with everything else. This module shows how to build a business case that survives scrutiny — and how to prove value.

Learning objectives

  • Structure an AI business case
  • Identify the full costs of an AI initiative
  • Estimate benefits with ranges and assumptions
  • Define business, adoption and quality metrics
  • Design pilots with clear success criteria

3.1 What a business case must show

A credible AI business case answers four questions: what will change, what it's worth, what it will cost (in full), and how we'll know — with the decision points for stopping or scaling.

3.2 Count the full cost

CostOften forgotten parts
TechnologyLicences or API usage (per user or per token), hosting, vector databases, monitoring tools
DataCleaning, labelling, integration, access controls
PeopleBuild team, subject-matter experts' time for testing and review, ongoing operations
ChangeTraining, process redesign, communication, temporary productivity dip
Risk & governanceAssessments, legal review, security testing, audits

Usage-based pricing scales with adoption: model costs carefully at the expected volume, not the pilot volume.

3.3 Estimate benefits honestly

Time saved is the most common benefit — and the most often overstated. Ask: what will people do with the time? Unless it's redeployed to valuable work, reduced overtime or hiring avoided, it isn't a financial saving. Use ranges (low / expected / high) rather than single numbers, and state your assumptions.

A simple worked example

200 support agents each spend 2 hours a day drafting replies. A pilot shows AI drafting cuts that by 30% with no drop in quality scores. That's 120 hours a day. If half of it absorbs growth that would otherwise need new hires, at a loaded cost of £30/hour over 230 working days, that's about £414,000 a year — before subtracting licence, integration and training costs.

3.4 Measure what matters

Define metrics at three levels before the pilot starts, with a baseline for each:

  • Business outcomes — cost per case, revenue, customer satisfaction, error rates
  • Process and adoption — active users, tasks completed with AI, time per task, override rates
  • Model quality and safety — accuracy on test sets, groundedness, harmful-output rate, latency, cost per request

Adoption without outcomes is a vanity metric; outcomes without quality metrics hide risk.

3.5 Design pilots to learn

  • A clear hypothesis and success threshold agreed up front ("≥ 25% faster with no fall in CSAT")
  • A comparison group or before/after baseline
  • Real users, real data, real volume — not a curated demo
  • A fixed time box (often 6–12 weeks) and a scale / iterate / stop decision at the end
Activity: a one-page business case

For your top use case from Module 2, write a one-page business case: the change, benefit range with assumptions, full cost (all five categories), three metrics with baselines, and the pilot's success threshold and time box.

See a worked example

Example — support drafting: Change: AI drafts replies, agents edit and send. Benefits: £250k–£500k/year (assumes 20–35% drafting time saved, half redeployed). Costs: licences £90k/yr, integration £60k one-off, training £20k, QA reviews 0.5 FTE. Metrics: handling time (baseline 7.5 min), CSAT (baseline 4.3/5), edit rate. Pilot: 30 agents, 8 weeks; scale if ≥ 25% faster and CSAT ≥ 4.2.

Key takeaways

  • Show what changes, its worth, its full cost and how you'll know.
  • Count data, people, change and governance costs — not just licences.
  • Time saved is only money saved if it's redeployed.
  • Measure outcomes, adoption and model quality together.
  • Pilots need a hypothesis, baseline, time box and decision.
