When to fine-tune

Fine-tuning is the most over-prescribed technique in generative AI. This module helps you decide when it's genuinely the right tool.

Learning objectives

  • Define fine-tuning
  • Apply the optimisation ladder
  • Identify what fine-tuning is good and bad at
  • Compare hosted and self-managed fine-tuning
  • Estimate the full cost of a fine-tuned model

1.1 What fine-tuning is

Fine-tuning means taking a pre-trained model and training it further on your own examples so it adapts its behaviour. The model's parameters change; afterwards, it behaves differently even with a short prompt.

1.2 Try these first

Fine-tuning is powerful but slow, costly to maintain and easy to get wrong. Most teams should climb this ladder one rung at a time, stopping as soon as quality is good enough:

  1. Better prompts — clear instructions, examples, structure (see Prompt Engineering).
  2. A better or different model — a stronger model, or a reasoning model, may simply solve it.
  3. Retrieval (RAG) — if the problem is missing knowledge (see RAG).
  4. Fine-tuning — if the problem is behaviour, format, style or efficiency, and you have good examples.

OpenAI's model optimisation guidance makes the same point: prompt engineering "may be all you need", and fine-tuning "can be a time-consuming process".

1.3 What fine-tuning is good — and bad — at

Good at

  • Consistent output formats and structure
  • Tone, style and brand voice
  • Narrow classification and extraction tasks
  • Following complex instructions reliably
  • Making a smaller, cheaper, faster model do one task well
  • Shorter prompts (fewer tokens per call)

Bad at

  • Teaching frequently changing facts — use RAG
  • Fixing a vague task definition — fix the task first
  • Small datasets of mixed quality
  • Guaranteeing accuracy — fine-tuned models still hallucinate
  • Anything you can't evaluate

1.4 Where you can fine-tune

OptionExamplesTrade-offs
Hosted fine-tuning from model providers and cloudsCloud AI platforms such as Amazon Bedrock, Google Vertex AI and Microsoft Azure AI Foundry offer fine-tuning for selected modelsEasy and managed; limited choice of models and methods; check data terms
Open-weight models you tune yourselfLlama, Mistral, Gemma, Qwen and others, using open-source libraries such as Hugging Face Transformers and PEFTFull control and privacy; you manage GPUs, serving and security
The landscape is shifting

Provider offerings change often. In 2026, OpenAI's documentation states that its fine-tuning platform is winding down and no longer open to new users — a reminder to check current availability, and to design so you're not locked into one vendor's tuning service.

1.5 The total cost of a fine-tuned model

  • Data: collecting, cleaning and labelling examples — usually the biggest cost.
  • Training: compute (GPUs) or platform fees.
  • Evaluation: test sets and repeated comparisons.
  • Serving: hosting a custom model may cost more than calling a shared one.
  • Maintenance: when the base model is updated or retired, you may need to re-tune and re-evaluate.
Try it: fine-tune or not?

(a) Your support bot doesn't know about this month's new product line. (b) Your product descriptions must always follow a precise 5-part template and brand voice, and prompting gets it right 80% of the time. (c) You want to classify 2 million emails a day cheaply.

Show suggested answers

(a) RAG — it's missing knowledge. (b) Good fine-tuning candidate — consistent format and style, after strengthening the prompt. (c) Strong candidate — fine-tune (or distil into) a small, cheap model for one narrow task.

Key takeaways

  • Fine-tuning changes the model's weights using your examples.
  • Try prompting, a better model and RAG first.
  • Fine-tuning suits format, style, narrow tasks and efficiency — not changing facts.
  • Provider offerings change; avoid lock-in.
  • Data and maintenance are usually the biggest costs.
