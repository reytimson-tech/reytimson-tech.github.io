The main types of AI

"AI" covers very different technologies. Knowing which kind you're dealing with tells you what it's good at — and how it's likely to go wrong.

Learning objectives

  • Recognise six families of AI used at work
  • Match each family to typical failures
  • Explain what large language models are and what multimodal means
  • Distinguish chatbots from agents, and AI users from AI builders

2.1 Six families of AI you'll meet at work

Most AI you'll meet falls into a handful of families. Knowing which one you're dealing with helps you predict how it might fail.

TypeWhat it doesWorkplace examplesTypical failure
Prediction & forecastingEstimates a number or likelihoodSales forecasts, demand planning, churn riskBreaks when conditions change (e.g. a pandemic)
Classification & scoringPuts things into categories or ranks themSpam filtering, CV screening, fraud alertsUnfair or inaccurate results for under-represented groups
RecommendationSuggests what you might want nextProduct suggestions, learning platforms, news feedsFilter bubbles; optimising for clicks, not value
Computer visionInterprets images and videoDocument scanning, quality inspection, ID checksPoor lighting, unusual angles, demographic accuracy gaps
Speech & languageTranscribes, translates and understands text or speechMeeting transcription, chatbots, sentiment analysisAccents, jargon, sarcasm
Generative AICreates new text, images, audio, video or codeDrafting, summarising, coding assistants, image creationHallucinations: plausible but false content

2.2 Generative AI and large language models

The generative AI tools most people use — ChatGPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google) and Microsoft Copilot — are built on large language models (LLMs). An LLM has been trained on vast amounts of text to predict what comes next. That simple-sounding skill, at enormous scale, lets it draft, summarise, translate, answer questions and write code.

Many of these models are now multimodal: they can take images, documents, audio and sometimes video as input, and produce more than text as output.

2.3 From chatbots to agents

A chatbot answers when you ask. An AI agent is given a goal and can take several steps to reach it, using tools: searching the web, reading files, sending messages, filling in forms or running code. Agents can save a lot of time, but they also raise the stakes — a mistake can turn into an action, not just a wrong sentence.

Example

Chatbot: "Draft a reply to this customer complaint." You read the draft and decide whether to send it.

Agent: "Handle new complaints in the inbox." The agent reads, categorises, drafts and sends replies, and issues refunds under £50. Now you need clear limits, logging and a human approval step for anything unusual.

2.4 AI you use vs AI you build

Most people at work are AI users (or, in EU AI Act terms, deployers): they use tools someone else built. A smaller group build or customise AI — training models, connecting LLMs to company data, or creating custom assistants. Both groups need AI literacy, but builders carry extra responsibilities for testing, documentation and safety.

Try it: classify your tools

Take three AI-powered tools from your work. For each, name its family from the table above and its most likely failure. Which one would cause the most harm if it went wrong — and who would notice?

Key takeaways

  • Prediction, classification, recommendation, vision, language and generative AI each fail in characteristic ways.
  • ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot are built on large language models, increasingly multimodal.
  • Agents turn AI mistakes into actions, so they need limits and approval steps.
  • Most staff are AI users; builders have extra duties for testing and safety.
