Making models smaller, faster and cheaper

At scale, every token costs money and time. This module covers the techniques that make AI affordable — many of which don't need fine-tuning at all.

Learning objectives

  • Explain quantisation and estimate memory savings
  • Describe distillation from teacher to student
  • Use pruning and smaller models appropriately
  • Apply routing, caching, batching and output limits
  • Estimate and compare running costs

6.1 Why optimise?

At scale, the cost and speed of each model call dominate. A model that's 10× cheaper and 3× faster can change what's economically possible. Fine-tuning is one route; there are several others — often simpler.

6.2 Quantisation

Models store parameters as numbers with a certain precision — commonly 16 bits each. Quantisation stores them with fewer bits (8 or 4), cutting memory and often speeding up inference, usually with a small loss in quality. A 7-billion-parameter model needs roughly 14 GB of memory at 16-bit, about 7 GB at 8-bit and about 3.5 GB at 4-bit (before overheads) — the difference between needing a data-centre GPU and running on a laptop.

6.3 Distillation

In distillation, a large, capable "teacher" model generates high-quality outputs for many examples of your task, and a small "student" model is fine-tuned on them. The student learns to imitate the teacher on that narrow task at a fraction of the cost. Check the teacher's terms of use permit this.

6.4 Pruning and smaller models

Pruning removes weights or whole components that contribute little. In practice, the easiest win is often simply choosing a smaller model from the same family and testing whether it's good enough — perhaps with a better prompt or a light fine-tune.

6.5 System-level optimisations

TechniqueHow it saves
Model routingSend easy requests to a small model and hard ones to a large model
Prompt cachingReuse the processed form of a long, repeated prompt prefix — supported by several providers to cut cost and latency
Shorter promptsFewer tokens per call; fine-tuning can replace long instructions and examples
BatchingProcess non-urgent requests in bulk, often at a discount
Response cachingStore answers to identical requests
Output limitsCap maximum output length; ask for concise formats

6.6 Do the maths

Python · rough monthly cost comparisondef monthly_cost(requests_per_day, in_tokens, out_tokens, price_in, price_out):
    # prices are per million tokens
    per_call = in_tokens / 1e6 * price_in + out_tokens / 1e6 * price_out
    return requests_per_day * 30 * per_call

# Illustrative US-dollar prices per million tokens — check your provider's current price list
large = monthly_cost(50_000, in_tokens=1_500, out_tokens=200, price_in=3.00, price_out=15.00)
small = monthly_cost(50_000, in_tokens=300,   out_tokens=200, price_in=0.25, price_out=1.25)
print(f"Large model, long prompt: ${large:,.0f}/month")
print(f"Small tuned model, short prompt: ${small:,.0f}/month")

Include the one-off costs (data, training, evaluation) and ongoing costs (hosting, re-tuning) to find the break-even point.

6.7 A decision checklist

  • Is the task well defined, with a test set and success criteria?
  • Have prompting, a better model and RAG been tried?
  • Do you have enough high-quality, lawful examples?
  • Does the tuned model beat baselines on quality, cost or speed?
  • Have general ability, safety and memorisation been re-checked?
  • Is there an owner and a plan for when the base model changes?

Key takeaways

  • Quantisation stores weights in fewer bits — roughly 16-bit → 4-bit cuts memory by about 4×.
  • Distillation teaches a small model to imitate a large one on a task.
  • Often the simplest win is a smaller model with a better prompt.
  • Routing, prompt caching, batching and output limits cut cost at system level.
  • Do the maths, including one-off and ongoing costs.
