The principles landscape

Governments, international bodies and AI companies have all published responsible AI principles. Here's how they compare — and what they share.

Learning objectives

  • Summarise the OECD, UNESCO and UK principles
  • Compare major AI companies' responsible AI frameworks
  • Identify six core principles common to most frameworks
  • Explain 'ethics washing' and what closes the principles-practice gap

2.1 International frameworks

Hundreds of AI ethics principles have been published. A few are especially influential:

FrameworkWhoCore principles
OECD AI Principles (2019, updated May 2024)Adopted by OECD members and other countries; basis for the G20 AI principlesInclusive growth and well-being; human rights and democratic values including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; accountability
UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021)Adopted by UNESCO's member statesHuman rights and dignity, environmental flourishing, diversity and inclusion, peace and justice — with policy action areas
UK AI regulation principles (2023 white paper)UK Government, applied by existing regulatorsSafety, security and robustness; appropriate transparency and explainability; fairness; accountability and governance; contestability and redress

2.3 The common core

Despite different wording, almost every framework converges on the same handful of ideas. This course uses six:

Fairness

AI should not create or reinforce unjust discrimination. (Module 3)

Transparency & explainability

People should know when AI is used and be able to understand relevant decisions. (Module 4)

Accountability

Identifiable people and organisations answer for AI systems and their effects. (Module 4)

Privacy

Personal data is protected and used lawfully and proportionately. (Module 5)

Safety & robustness

Systems work reliably, resist misuse and fail safely. (Module 5)

Human agency & oversight

People stay in meaningful control, and AI supports rather than undermines autonomy. (Module 5)

2.4 From principles to practice

Principles are easy to publish and hard to live by. Critics call the gap "ethics washing": impressive values with no change in practice. What closes the gap:

  • Concrete requirements derived from each principle (e.g. "test performance across demographic groups before launch")
  • Roles with responsibility and authority
  • Review points in the product lifecycle where work can be paused or stopped
  • Tools for testing, documentation and monitoring
  • Transparency about what was done — and what went wrong
Try it: compare frameworks

Pick two organisations' principles from the table (or your own organisation's, if it has some). Which of the six core ideas does each cover? What's missing? What evidence would convince you they're applied in practice?

Key takeaways

  • The OECD Principles (updated 2024) and UNESCO Recommendation are global reference points.
  • The UK applies five cross-sector principles through existing regulators.
  • Company frameworks differ in wording but converge on a common core.
  • Principles only matter with requirements, roles, review points, tools and transparency.
