Operating model, governance and risk

How you organise determines how fast you scale — and how safely. This module covers operating models, governance and leadership risks.

Learning objectives

  • Compare AI operating models
  • Describe a centre of excellence's responsibilities
  • Design risk-tiered governance
  • Identify strategic AI risks and responses

5.1 Organising for AI

ModelHow it worksTrade-off
CentralisedOne central AI team builds for everyoneConsistent and expert, but a bottleneck far from the business
DecentralisedEach business unit does its own AIFast and close to need, but duplicated effort and uneven risk control
Hub and spokeA central hub (centre of excellence) sets standards, platforms and guardrails; spokes in business units deliver use casesBalances speed and control — the most common choice at scale

5.2 What the hub does

  • Owns shared platforms, approved tools and reusable components
  • Sets standards for evaluation, security, data and documentation
  • Runs the use-case pipeline and portfolio reporting
  • Provides expertise and coaching to spoke teams
  • Coordinates AI literacy and training

5.3 Governance that enables

Good governance makes it easier to do the right thing quickly. Tier oversight by risk: a light-touch checklist for low-risk internal productivity uses, deeper review for customer-facing or high-impact systems. Core elements:

  • An AI policy and acceptable-use rules for staff
  • An inventory of AI systems with owners and risk ratings
  • Risk assessment and approval before deployment
  • Monitoring, incident reporting and periodic review
  • Clear accountability — a named owner for every system

The full approach is in AI Governance and Responsible AI.

5.4 Strategic risks for leaders

RiskLeadership response
Doing nothing — competitors move fasterA funded portfolio with clear priorities
Shadow AI — staff use unapproved tools with company dataProvide approved tools, clear rules and training
Reputational harm from errors or biasRisk-tiered review, testing, human oversight
Regulatory breachInventory, risk classification, legal involvement early
Vendor dependencyMulti-model design, exit terms, data portability
Workforce anxietyHonest communication, reskilling, involving staff in redesign

5.5 The board's role

Boards should understand the AI strategy, risk appetite and major systems; receive regular reporting on value delivered and incidents; and make sure accountability is clear. Directors don't need to be technical, but they do need to be AI-literate enough to ask good questions.

Activity: design your operating model

Sketch an operating model for AI in your organisation: hub or no hub, which teams are spokes, what the hub owns, and the three governance controls you'd introduce first.

See a worked example

Example — 800-person company: A small hub (4 people) inside the digital team owns the approved assistant, the model platform and standards. Spokes: customer service, finance, marketing, each with an AI champion. First controls: acceptable-use policy, AI system inventory, risk checklist before any customer-facing launch.

Key takeaways

  • Hub and spoke balances speed and control.
  • The hub owns platforms, standards, pipeline and coaching.
  • Tier governance by risk so low-risk uses move fast.
  • Shadow AI is best tackled with approved tools and training.
  • Boards need AI literacy, reporting and clear accountability.
